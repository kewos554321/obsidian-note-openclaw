**3. 對自住者建議**

**4. 對投資者建議**

#### 📚 Today's Concept: Market Capitalization tiers (Large/Mid/Small cap)

What it is: Market capitalization is a company's share price multiplied by its total shares outstanding, and tiers group companies by size: large cap (typically over $10 billion), mid cap ($2-10 billion), and small cap (under $2 billion). These thresholds are conventions, not laws, and vary slightly by source.

Why it matters: Cap tier tells you a lot about a stock's risk, liquidity, and growth profile before you read a single financial statement, so it helps you match holdings to your risk tolerance and position sizing.

Example: A stock trades at $50 with 400 million shares outstanding: $50 × 400M = $20 billion market cap, a large cap. If the price fell to $4, the cap would be $1.6 billion, dropping it into small-cap territory. Note that price alone tells you nothing; a $500 stock can be small cap if share count is tiny.

Rule of thumb: Small caps offer more growth but thinner liquidity and higher volatility; never size a small-cap position like a large cap, and treat a cap-tier downgrade as a signal to re-check your thesis, not an automatic sell.

**連結:** https://leetcode.com/problems/last-stone-weight/
> 📅 **Today's Daily Challenge:** #3742 Maximum Number of Items From Sale I [Medium] — Tags: Array, Dynamic Programming, Greedy — https://leetcode.com/problems/maximum-number-of-items-from-sale-i/

# 1046. Last Stone Weight

**Problem Type:** Max-Heap / Greedy Simulation

**Key Insight:** Always smash the two heaviest stones — Python's `heapq` is a min-heap, so negate values to simulate a max-heap. Repeat until ≤1 stone remains.

**Approach:**
1. Build a max-heap by pushing `-x` for every stone.
2. While heap has ≥2 elements: pop two (negate back), compute `diff = y - x`.
3. If `diff > 0`, push `-diff` back.
4. Return `-heap[0]` if heap non-empty, else `0`.

**Python3 Solution:**
```python
import heapq

class Solution:
    def lastStoneWeight(self, stones: List[int]) -> int:
        heap = [-s for s in stones]
        heapq.heapify(heap)
        while len(heap) > 1:
            y = -heapq.heappop(heap)
            x = -heapq.heappop(heap)
            if y != x:
                heapq.heappush(heap, -(y - x))
        return -heap[0] if heap else 0
```

**Complexity:** Time O(n log n) | Space O(n)

**Blind 75 Note:** Canonical "max-heap via negation" problem. Teaches the Python idiom of negating for max-heap behavior — a pattern you'll reuse in Kth Largest, Task Scheduler, IPO, and Merge K Sorted Lists. Also reinforces greedy simulation: always take the two largest.

**Contest Tips:**
- **Negation is the trick** — Python has no built-in max-heap. Don't forget to negate on push *and* pop.
- **`heapify` is O(n)** — faster than n pushes. Use it.
- **Edge cases:** single stone → return it; all stones equal → return 0; empty input (not in constraints but good habit) → 0.
- **Don't sort each iteration** — that's O(n² log n). The heap gives O(log n) per operation.
- **`y != x` check** — if equal, both vanish; only push when different.

#### 📷 Today's Concept: Light — Using Reflectors and Diffusers

**What it is:** Reflectors bounce existing light back into shadows to fill them, while diffusers scatter harsh light into a soft, even glow. Together they let you shape natural or artificial light without buying a single strobe.

**Why it matters:** A $20 5-in-1 reflector/diffuser transforms harsh midday sun into flattering portrait light and lifts shadow detail in street and landscape shots. It's the cheapest way to make light look intentional.

**How to apply it:**
1. **Diffuse first:** Hold a 1-stop diffuser between the sun and your subject (or have them stand in open shade) to soften hard shadows before adding fill.
2. **Position the reflector opposite the light source** — silver for punchy contrast, white for gentle fill, gold for warm skin tones.
3. **Watch the catchlights:** Angle the reflector until you see a clean highlight in your subject's eyes, then fine-tune distance for intensity (closer = stronger fill).
4. **For cinematic video,** flag or diffuse a window and use a white reflector as a subtle fill from the shadow side — keep the ratio around 3:1 for mood.
5. **For landscapes,** use a diffuser to tame blown highlights in bright skies, or a reflector to bounce warm light onto foreground rocks/flowers.

**Sony A7C tip:** Enable **Zebra** (Display menu) set to 95% to spot clipped highlights on skin when working with reflectors in bright sun — and pair with the compact 35mm f/1.8 for handheld one-person reflector work.

**Common mistake:** Bouncing too much light and killing all shadow depth. Start with white, not silver, and pull the reflector back until shadows still read — fill, don't flatten.

#### 📚 Today's Concept: Circuit Breaker Pattern

**What it is:** A stability pattern that wraps calls to a failing dependency and "trips open" after repeated failures, failing fast instead of hammering a dead service. It transitions through closed → open → half-open states to detect recovery.

**When to use it:** When calling remote services (APIs, databases, third parties) where failures can cascade and exhaust threads/connections. E.g., your checkout service calling a flaky payment gateway—without it, slow timeouts pile up and take down checkout too.

**Example:**
```python
breaker = CircuitBreaker(fail_threshold=5, reset_timeout=30)

@breaker
def charge_card(order):
    return payment_api.charge(order)

# After 5 failures, calls raise CircuitOpenError immediately
# for 30s, then a trial call checks recovery.
```

**Gotcha:** People assume the breaker *retries* or *fixes* the call—it doesn't. It only short-circuits. Also, forgetting to tune thresholds/timeouts per dependency means you either trip too eagerly (false positives on transient blips) or too late (no protection). Pair it with fallbacks and monitoring, not as a standalone fix.
